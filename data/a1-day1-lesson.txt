VoiceLingo — Sample Lesson Manuscript
Level: A1 (Beginner)
Topic: Greetings and introducing yourself
Format assumption: audio-only, hands-free interaction (e.g. driving) — the
student cannot look at a screen, so nothing in this lesson depends on
visuals, text, or gestures. Everything must work by ear alone.
Session length: ~15 minutes, one short lesson per day.
Student: Alex (this is Alex's 1st daily session — first time meeting the
teacher).

---

TEACHER: ¡Hola! I'm Marisol, I'll be doing these with you every day —
just fifteen minutes, and we build a little bit each time. Today's short:
how to greet someone and introduce yourself. That's it. Ready?

STUDENT: Ready.

TEACHER: First thing — Spanish greetings change depending on the time of
day. Listen: buenos días.

STUDENT: Buenos días.

TEACHER: That's for morning. Buenas tardes — for afternoon.

STUDENT: Buenas tardes.

TEACHER: And buenas noches — evening, or night.

STUDENT: Buenas noches.

TEACHER: Notice "buenos" only goes with "días" — everything else gets
"buenas." Don't worry about why, just remember that pairing. Quick check —
what time is it where you are right now?

STUDENT: Like 4pm.

TEACHER: So which greeting?

STUDENT: Buenas tardes.

TEACHER: Exacto. Say it to me like you just picked up the phone and it's
4pm.

STUDENT: Buenas tardes.

TEACHER: Buenas tardes. ¿Cómo estás? — that means "how are you," casual,
with someone your age or someone you know. Say it back to me.

STUDENT: ¿Cómo estás?

TEACHER: Estoy bien, gracias. "Estoy bien" — "I'm doing well." Try that
part on its own.

STUDENT: Estoy bien.

TEACHER: Bien. Now if you want to ask me back — "¿y tú?" — "and you?" All
together now: I'll ask, you answer and ask back.

TEACHER: ¿Cómo estás?

STUDENT: Estoy bien, gracias. ¿Y tú?

TEACHER: Muy bien, gracias. — Perfecto, that's a real exchange already.

STUDENT: That was faster than I thought it'd be.

TEACHER: That's the idea. Okay, next piece — telling someone your name.
In Spanish you don't say "I am Alex," you say "me llamo Alex" — literally
closer to "I call myself Alex," but don't translate it in your head, just
treat it as one chunk. Say your name with it.

STUDENT: Me llamo Alex.

TEACHER: Bien. And to ask someone else's name: "¿cómo te llamas?" Ask me.

STUDENT: ¿Cómo te llamas?

TEACHER: Me llamo Marisol. ¿Y tú, cómo te llamas?

STUDENT: Me llamo Alex.

TEACHER: One last piece and then we put it all together. After someone
tells you their name, you can say "mucho gusto" — nice to meet you. Try
it.

STUDENT: Mucho gusto.

TEACHER: There's also "encantado" — same meaning, a little more formal —
but that one changes if you're a man or a woman speaking. You'd say
"encantado."

STUDENT: Encantado.

TEACHER: If I said it, since I'm a woman, I'd say "encantada" — with an
A. You'll see that -o/-a pattern again later, it's not just this word.
For now, just yours: encantado.

STUDENT: Encantado.

TEACHER: Okay — full thing, start to finish. Pretend we've never met.
I'll go first.

TEACHER: ¡Buenos días!

STUDENT: ¡Buenos días! ¿Cómo estás?

TEACHER: Estoy bien, gracias. ¿Y tú?

STUDENT: Estoy bien también.

TEACHER: Oh, "también" — "also" — I didn't teach you that one, where'd
that come from?

STUDENT: I think I've heard it before somewhere.

TEACHER: Steal it, I don't mind. Keep going, you're doing great.

TEACHER: Me llamo Marisol. ¿Cómo te llamas?

STUDENT: Me llamo Alex. Mucho gusto.

TEACHER: Encantada, Alex.

TEACHER: One more time, but I'll change the time of day on you — try to
catch it.

TEACHER: ¡Buenas noches!

STUDENT: *(catches it)* — ¡Buenas noches! ¿Cómo estás?

TEACHER: Muy bien, gracias. Me llamo Marisol, ¿cómo te llamas?

STUDENT: Me llamo Alex. Encantado.

TEACHER: Mucho gusto, Alex. — Perfecto. That's a full conversation, no
hesitation on that second round.

STUDENT: Yeah, that one felt easier.

TEACHER: We're almost at fifteen minutes, so quick recap: the three
greetings, depending on time of day?

STUDENT: Buenos días, buenas tardes, buenas noches.

TEACHER: ¿Y cómo preguntas el nombre de alguien?

STUDENT: ¿Cómo te llamas?

TEACHER: ¿Y cómo respondes?

STUDENT: Me llamo... Alex.

TEACHER: *(laughs)* Sí, todavía te llamas Alex. For homework — say
"buenos días" or "buenas tardes" to someone this week, in real life,
doesn't matter if they understand you or not. Tomorrow we'll build on
this — asking where someone's from. Same time tomorrow?

STUDENT: Same time works.

TEACHER: Perfecto. ¡Buenas noches, Alex!

STUDENT: Buenas noches, Marisol. Thanks.

---

Notes for future lesson design:

- First-ever session opens with the teacher naming herself and stating
  the format (15 minutes, daily, cumulative) — there's no prior lesson to
  reference yet, so rapport has to be built from zero within the same
  short window as the actual teaching.
- New material is introduced one small piece at a time (greeting choice,
  then "cómo estás," then "me llamo," then "mucho gusto"), each
  immediately looped back into a two-line exchange before moving on —
  never more than one new chunk sitting unpracticed at a time.
- The "buenos vs. buenas" and "encantado vs. encantada" gender/number
  quirks are flagged but explicitly deferred ("you'll see that pattern
  again later") rather than explained in grammatical depth — day 1 for a
  true beginner should not carry a grammar lecture.
- The roleplay changes exactly one variable between repetitions (time of
  day) so the student has to actually listen rather than recite from
  memory, while everything else stays fixed as a scaffold.
- Homework seeds the opening question of the next day's lesson (see
  a1-day2-lesson.txt), so each session's close and the following
  session's open form one continuous thread rather than resetting daily.
