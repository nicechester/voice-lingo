VoiceLingo — Sample Lesson Manuscript
Level: A1 (Beginner)
Topic: Greetings, introducing yourself, saying where you're from
Format assumption: audio-only, hands-free interaction (e.g. driving) — the
student cannot look at a screen, so nothing in this lesson depends on
visuals, text, or gestures. Everything must work by ear alone.
Session length: ~15 minutes, one short lesson per day.
Student: Alex (this is Alex's 2nd daily session; day 1 covered greetings
and giving your name).

---

TEACHER: ¡Hola, Alex! Quick thing first — did you say "buenos días" to
anyone this week, like I asked?

STUDENT: I did, actually. Said it to my barista yesterday morning.

TEACHER: ¿Y qué dijo?

STUDENT: She just said it back and smiled, I think she thought it was a
little random.

TEACHER: That counts! Okay — since it's morning for you right now, greet
me properly.

STUDENT: Buenos días, profesora. ¿Cómo estás?

TEACHER: Estoy muy bien, gracias. ¿Y tú?

STUDENT: Estoy bien también.

TEACHER: Bien. And if I don't know you yet?

STUDENT: Um — me llamo Alex. Mucho gusto.

TEACHER: Encantada. — Good, that's automatic now, I didn't even have to
prompt you for "cómo te llamas." Today we're adding one thing on top of
that: where you're from. Ready?

STUDENT: Ready.

TEACHER: The question is "¿De dónde eres?" — "Where are you from?" Say it
back to me.

STUDENT: ¿De dónde eres?

TEACHER: Bien. And to answer, you say "Soy de" plus the place. Soy de
México. Now you — where are you from?

STUDENT: Soy de... Chicago.

TEACHER: Soy de Chicago. Perfecto, city names mostly don't change. If
someone asks your country, same pattern — soy de los Estados Unidos.

STUDENT: Soy de los Estados Unidos.

TEACHER: Muy bien. Now — quick trap, listen closely. If I ask YOU, I say
"¿de dónde ERES?" — with an S, because I'm asking about "tú." But if I'm
telling you about someone else, like my friend, I'd say "¿de dónde ES?" —
no S. "Eres" is you, "es" is he or she. Don't worry about producing that
part yet, just recognize it when you hear it. ¿De dónde es tu papá?

STUDENT: Uh... he's from Ireland — is de Irlanda?

TEACHER: ¡Sí! Es de Irlanda. You didn't need "eres" there, good instinct.

STUDENT: Okay that makes more sense now.

TEACHER: Good. Let's put it all together — full conversation, like we're
strangers meeting. I'll start.

TEACHER: ¡Buenas tardes!

STUDENT: ¡Buenas tardes! ¿Cómo estás?

TEACHER: Estoy bien, gracias. ¿Y tú?

STUDENT: Estoy bien también. Me llamo Alex.

TEACHER: Mucho gusto, Alex. Me llamo Marisol.

STUDENT: Encantado. ¿De dónde eres?

TEACHER: Soy de México. ¿Y tú, de dónde eres?

STUDENT: Soy de Chicago, en los Estados Unidos.

TEACHER: ¡Qué bien! — Notice you added "en los Estados Unidos" on your
own, I didn't teach you that connector, that was just you being
resourceful.

STUDENT: I panicked and it came out, honestly.

TEACHER: That's usually how it works. Okay, we're at about ten minutes —
let's do that same conversation one more time, but I want you to change
something. Different time of day, different city, make up a name if you
want.

TEACHER: ¡Buenos días!

STUDENT: ¡Buenos días! ¿Cómo estás?

TEACHER: Estoy bien. Me llamo Marisol, ¿cómo te llamas?

STUDENT: Me llamo... Roberto. Mucho gusto.

TEACHER: Encantada, Roberto. ¿De dónde eres?

STUDENT: Soy de Los Ángeles.

TEACHER: Perfecto. See how none of that took you long to produce that
time? That's the goal every day — not new grammar rules, just less
hesitation.

STUDENT: Yeah, it's coming faster.

TEACHER: Bueno, casi se acaba el tiempo — we're almost out of time. Quick
recap: how do you ask where someone's from?

STUDENT: ¿De dónde eres?

TEACHER: ¿Y cómo respondes?

STUDENT: Soy de... wherever I'm from.

TEACHER: Exacto. For tomorrow — I want you to actually introduce yourself
to someone this week using all of it: greeting, name, and where you're
from, real person, doesn't matter who. Same time tomorrow?

STUDENT: Same time works.

TEACHER: Perfecto. ¡Buenas noches, Alex! Bien hecho hoy.

STUDENT: Buenas noches, profesora. Thanks.

---

Notes for future lesson design:

- Every teacher line must be understandable by ear alone — no reliance on
  spelling, punctuation cues (¿ ¡), or seeing text on screen.
- New material introduced per session: exactly one question/answer pair
  ("¿De dónde eres?" / "Soy de...") plus one passive-recognition-only
  point (eres vs. es) — small enough to fit a 15-minute session without
  cramming.
- Repetition comes from replaying the same roleplay with one variable
  changed (time of day, name, city), not from introducing new content
  twice.
- The teacher tracks elapsed time out loud ("we're at about ten minutes,"
  "casi se acaba el tiempo") since there's no visible progress bar or
  timer for the student to check.
- The teacher notices and rewards language the student produces outside
  the lesson's own scripted vocabulary (e.g. "en los Estados Unidos" or
  "también"), rather than only accepting the expected phrase.
- Correction happens by continuing the conversation and letting the
  correct form resurface naturally, not by stopping to say "wrong, try
  again."
- Homework is one small, concrete, real-world action tied to today's
  content, not a review packet.
